The anatomy of a great onboarding journey

Breaking down what separates a forgettable welcome sequence from one that actually converts users and sets the foundation for long-term retention.

Onboarding is everything

If I had to pick one part of the customer lifecycle to invest in above all others, it would be onboarding. Every retention strategy, every win-back campaign, every loyalty program — they all work better when the foundation is solid. And the foundation is set in the first few days after signup.

I've built and optimized onboarding journeys across different product types — from EdTech platforms to SaaS tools to apps targeting consumers across Latin America. And I keep coming back to the same set of principles.

The goal isn't to inform. It's to activate.

Most onboarding sequences are built like product tours. They explain features. They show screenshots. They tell users what they can do. And then they wonder why nobody does anything.

The problem is that information doesn't change behavior. Experience does. The goal of onboarding isn't to explain your product — it's to get the user to their first moment of genuine value as quickly as possible.

What does that moment look like for your product? Define it. That's your North Star for the entire onboarding journey. Every message, every touchpoint, every piece of content should be asking: does this move the user toward that moment?

The structure that works

Here's how I typically structure an onboarding journey:

  • Welcome (Day 0): Confirm the decision. Remind them why they signed up. Set expectations. Keep it short.
  • First value trigger (Day 1–2): Guide them to the single most important action. One CTA. Make it easy.
  • Momentum (Day 3–5): Celebrate progress. Introduce the next step. Use behavioral triggers, not time delays.
  • Early retention check (Day 7–10): Check for activation. If they haven't hit the milestone, re-engage. If they have, deepen the relationship.

Behavioral triggers over time delays

One of the biggest upgrades you can make to any onboarding journey is switching from time-based to behavior-based messaging. Instead of sending "Day 3 email" to everyone, send it when someone has — or hasn't — done a specific thing.

This requires decent event tracking, but it doesn't have to be complicated. Start with the one or two actions that predict long-term retention in your product. Track those. Build branches around them. The improvement in performance is almost always significant.

What most teams skip

The thing I see skipped most often is the "not activated" branch. Teams build a beautiful journey for users who do everything right. But what happens to the users who don't open the first email? Who sign up but never complete the profile? Who complete step one but stop there?

These users need a different conversation. They're not lost yet — but they will be if you keep sending them the same messages as your engaged users.

Build the "at-risk" branch. It's often where the highest-impact interventions live.
