A/B testing: doing it right vs doing it fast

How to design experiments that give you real, actionable answers — not just numbers that look good in a presentation but teach you nothing.

The testing culture problem

Almost every marketing team I've worked with or talked to claims they "do A/B testing." And they do — technically. They split audiences, they compare variants, they look at which one performed better.

But very few of them are actually learning anything. They're generating results, not insights. And there's a big difference.

Why most tests don't teach you anything

Here are the patterns I see most often:

  • Testing too many things at once. If you change the subject line, the CTA, and the send time simultaneously, you don't know which change drove the result.
  • Stopping too early. The test "wins" after two days with a 52% vs 48% open rate. That's not statistically significant — it's noise.
  • Optimizing for the wrong metric. A higher open rate doesn't always mean better performance. If your goal is conversion, measure conversion.
  • Testing without a hypothesis. If you don't know what you're trying to learn before you start, you'll rationalize any result after the fact.

The one rule that changes everything

Start with a hypothesis. Not "let's test two subject lines and see what wins" — that's not a hypothesis, that's a coin flip with more steps. A real hypothesis sounds like: "We believe that using the user's first name in the subject line will increase open rates because it feels more personal and relevant in a crowded inbox."

When you have a hypothesis, you know what you're testing and why. You can design the test properly. And when you have results, you can actually interpret them — not just declare a winner.

Statistical significance isn't optional

I know it sounds tedious, but this is the thing that separates real testing from theater. Before you run a test, decide:

  • What's your minimum detectable effect? (How big does the difference need to be to actually matter to your business?)
  • What's your required confidence level? (Typically 95% — meaning you'd only see this result by chance 5% of the time.)
  • What sample size do you need to reach statistical significance at those levels?

Run the test until you hit that sample size. Not until Monday. Not until the end of the week. Until the math says you can trust the result.

Build a learning library

The best CRM teams I know treat their test results like a library. Every test gets documented: the hypothesis, the setup, the results, the interpretation, and what was done with the finding.

Over time, this becomes one of the most valuable assets in your marketing operation. You stop re-learning the same lessons. You build on what worked. And when someone asks "why do we do it this way?", you have an actual answer.
