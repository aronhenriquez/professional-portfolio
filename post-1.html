Why most companies fail at lifecycle marketing (and how to fix it)

The most common mistake I see teams make — and the framework I use to think about the full customer journey from acquisition to advocacy.

The pattern I keep seeing

After years working in CRM and lifecycle marketing across different companies and markets, I've noticed a pattern. Most teams don't fail because of bad campaigns or wrong tools. They fail because they're solving for the wrong thing.

They optimize for opens. For clicks. For short-term conversion rates. And then they wonder why their retention numbers don't move, or why users who converted with a great promo churn a month later.

The problem isn't execution — it's orientation. They're looking at channels when they should be looking at the customer.

The channel-first trap

Most CRM teams organize themselves around channels: the email team, the push team, the SMS team. Each has their own metrics, their own calendar, their own goals. And because each team is optimizing their own channel, nobody is looking at what the user is actually experiencing across all of them.

The user gets an onboarding email on day 1. A push notification on day 2. A promotional SMS on day 3. Another email on day 4. None of these teams know what the others sent. The user feels bombarded and confused. They churn — not because the product was bad, but because the communication was.

Customer-first thinking

The fix isn't complicated, but it requires a shift in how you think about your work. Instead of asking "what should we send via email this week?", ask "what does a user in this stage of their journey need to hear right now, and what's the best way to reach them?"

This means:

  • Mapping your customer journey before you plan any campaigns
  • Defining clear lifecycle stages with measurable entry and exit criteria
  • Creating communication rules that prevent channel overlap and message fatigue
  • Measuring success at the journey level, not just the campaign level

The framework I use

I think about the customer lifecycle in five stages: Acquire → Activate → Engage → Retain → Recover. Each stage has a different goal, a different emotional state for the user, and a different job for marketing to do.

The mistake most teams make is jumping straight to "Engage" and "Retain" without properly investing in "Activate." If a user doesn't have their first meaningful experience with your product, no retention campaign will save them.

Activation is the most underfunded stage in most CRM strategies. And it's usually the highest-leverage one.

What to do this week

If you want to start making this shift, begin with a simple audit. Map out every automated message your company sends. Look at them all together, from the user's point of view. Ask yourself: does this sequence tell a coherent story? Does it guide the user toward something valuable? Or does it feel like a series of unrelated broadcasts?

Most teams are surprised by what they find. But that audit is the starting point for building something that actually works.
